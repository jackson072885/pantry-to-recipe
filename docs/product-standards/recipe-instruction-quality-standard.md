# Recipe Instruction Quality Standard

## Purpose

Pantry to Plate is not a generic recipe app. It is a pantry-first dinner decision engine that helps a normal person answer:

> What can I cook tonight with what I already have?

A recipe is not good enough just because it has ingredients and basic steps. The instructions must be clear enough for a tired, busy, or uncertain cook to follow without needing hidden cooking knowledge.

The goal is not to make every recipe long. The goal is to make every recipe calm, practical, and confidence-building.

## Core Principle

Recipe instructions should sound like a steady person standing beside the user in the kitchen.

They should explain:

- what tool or pan to use
- how much heat to use
- how long each major step takes
- what the food should look, smell, or feel like
- how to know meat or fish is safely cooked
- when to lower heat, stir, drain, cover, rest, or serve
- what common mistake to avoid when useful

## Beginner-Safe Instruction Requirements

Each recipe should include most of the following when relevant.

### 1. Cookware or Vessel Guidance

Tell the user what to cook in.

Good examples:

- Use a large skillet over medium-high heat.
- Fill a medium pot about 2/3 full with water.
- Spread everything in a lightly greased baking dish.
- Use a soup pot or Dutch oven so the ingredients have room to simmer.

Avoid vague wording like:

- Cook in a pan.
- Boil the pasta.
- Bake until done.

### 2. Heat Level

Every stovetop recipe should clearly state the heat level.

Common heat language:

- low
- medium-low
- medium
- medium-high
- high

Good examples:

- Heat a large skillet over medium-high heat.
- Reduce the heat to medium-low so the sauce simmers instead of boiling hard.
- Cook over low heat for 1 minute after adding the cheese so it melts without breaking.

### 3. Water, Oil, Salt, and Liquid Guidance

When water, oil, salt, broth, or sauce liquid matters, give a practical amount.

Good examples:

- Fill a medium pot about 2/3 full with water.
- Add about 1 tablespoon of salt to the pasta water.
- Add 1 tablespoon oil to the skillet.
- Add 2 cups water, or enough to just cover the rice and vegetables.
- Add a splash of pasta water if the sauce gets too thick.

Acceptable softer language:

- a small splash of oil
- a good pinch of salt
- enough broth to barely cover the vegetables

Avoid vague wording when it leaves the user guessing:

- salted water
- a little oil
- enough water
- some broth

### 4. Timing Ranges

Major cooking steps should include approximate time windows.

Good examples:

- Cook for 5 to 7 minutes until the chicken is browned and no pink remains in the center.
- Simmer for 8 to 10 minutes until the sauce thickens enough to coat a spoon.
- Bake for 20 to 25 minutes until the edges bubble and the top is browned in spots.

Avoid:

- Cook until done.
- Simmer until ready.
- Bake until hot.

### 5. Doneness Cues

Every recipe should tell the user how to recognize when the food is ready.

Use visual, texture, smell, or safety cues.

Good examples:

- Chicken is done when the thickest pieces have no pink in the center or reach 165°F.
- Fish is done when it flakes easily with a fork and looks opaque in the center.
- Rice is done when the grains are tender and the liquid is absorbed.
- Pasta should be tender but still have a little bite.
- The sauce is ready when it coats the spoon and looks glossy.

### 6. Sequence Clarity

Make the order of actions obvious.

Good examples:

- While the pasta cooks, heat the skillet.
- Add the garlic during the last 30 seconds so it does not burn.
- Drain the pasta before adding it to the sauce.
- Rest the casserole for 5 minutes before serving so it holds together.

Avoid cramming too many actions into one unclear sentence.

### 7. Beginner Safety and Mistake Prevention

Add small warnings when they prevent common failure.

Good examples:

- Stir the pasta once after adding it so it does not stick.
- Lower the heat if the sauce bubbles hard.
- Add garlic near the end so it smells fragrant but does not burn.
- Do not crowd the skillet; cook in batches if needed.
- Let the pan heat first so the fish sears instead of steaming.

### 8. Required vs Optional Ingredient Clarity

Instructions must respect required and optional ingredients.

If an ingredient is optional, the recipe should not depend on it to work.

Good examples:

- Add the parmesan if using.
- Top with green onion if you have it.
- Serve over rice if using, or eat it as a skillet meal.

Bad examples:

- A recipe lists rice as optional but the instructions require serving over rice.
- A recipe lists cream as optional but the sauce only works if cream is present.
- A required ingredient is never used in the instructions.

### 9. Pantry Truth and Cookability

Instructions must not hide missing ingredients or fake certainty.

If exact quantity matters, the recipe should be clear.

Good examples:

- Use about 1 cup rice with 2 cups water unless your package says otherwise.
- Use enough chicken to cover the bottom of the skillet in one layer.
- If the sauce looks dry, add 1 to 2 tablespoons water and stir.

Instructions should support the app’s cookability states:

- ready to cook
- almost there
- check amount
- missing required ingredient

### 10. Tone

Use plain, calm, practical language.

Good tone:

- direct
- helpful
- warm
- specific
- not fancy
- not chef-y
- not overly long

Avoid:

- vague chef language
- unnecessary storytelling
- intimidating terms without explanation
- long paragraphs that bury the action
